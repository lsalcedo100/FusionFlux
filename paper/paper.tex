\begin{abstract}
Confinement scaling laws are fitted to existing tokamaks and used to size ones
not yet built, so what matters is accuracy on an unseen device. On the
6228-row file distributed as the standard analysis set of the ITPA Global
H-mode Confinement Database (DB5.2.3; 16 devices), cross-validation that holds
out discharges gives a random forest 29\% lower log-RMSE than a refitted power
law. Holding out whole devices reverses the
ranking: the forest is worse than the power law on all 11 devices with enough
data to score. The reversal survives, on most devices, with stored energy as the target and,
in a diagnostic not blind to the target, in dimensionless groups carrying less
device identity.
% ishcdb:begin
It reproduces on all 7 eligible devices of the stellarator-heliotron
confinement database, in an analysis fixed before the data were downloaded.
% ishcdb:end
At an internal size cut matching ITER's $1.82$-fold step in major radius,
with one device supplying 96\% of the held-out rows, 90\% split-conformal
intervals calibrated on held-out discharges cover 3\% and 0.3\% of those rows
for the two tree ensembles at widths that change by under 1.5\%: width does
not diagnose the loss of coverage. Across that cut, Connor--Taylor
constraints on the exponents and a Gaussian process with a linear kernel term
both reduce the error, and an ablation that varies only the long-range mean
is consistent with loss of a continuing trend as one contributor, beyond
flexibility alone. Four reporting checks follow, three without extra fitting.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A tokamak ignites when the alpha heating from its own fusion reactions covers
everything the plasma loses. The energy confinement time $\tau_E$ sets that
loss rate, so it is a principal input to how large a next-step device has to
be. Empirical scaling remains a principal tool for projecting it to a device
that does not exist yet\cite{ipb98}: a power law in the engineering parameters,
\begin{equation}
\tau_E = C\, I_p^{a_1} B_t^{a_2} \bar{n}_e^{a_3} P^{a_4} R^{a_5}
         \epsilon^{a_6} \kappa^{a_7} M^{a_8},
\label{eq:powerlaw}
\end{equation}
fitted by least squares across discharges from the multi-machine confinement
database. In log space Eq.~\eqref{eq:powerlaw} is a linear model. The
reference fit is IPB98(y,2)\cite{ipb98}, which served as the reference
H-mode confinement scaling in ITER's design and performance projections. The database is still maintained and republished\cite{hdb5}, and the 2007
revision of the ITER physics basis kept IPB98(y,2) as the reference H-mode
scaling\cite{doyle07}.

What such a law is used for is an extrapolation, and a large one. ITER's major
radius is 1.82 times that of the largest machine in the database, and
SPARC\cite{creely} is designed for a toroidal field 2.1 times the largest
recorded in it. First-principles and reduced transport models offer a second
route to the same number, and the machine learning in that branch trains
neural surrogates to reproduce a physics model fast enough to run inside a
simulation\cite{citrin,meneghini}; SPARC's core performance is projected that
way\cite{sparccore} alongside the empirical route. Nothing below concerns that
branch. The empirical law is still fitted, published, quoted as a design point
and used to benchmark the physics-based projections, and this paper is about
how empirical models are chosen.

Replacing Eq.~\eqref{eq:powerlaw} with a flexible regressor is an old idea.
Allen and Bishop\cite{allen} fitted a neural network to confinement data in
1992 and found that it beat linear regression on an in-distribution score.
Machine learning has since been productive in disruption
prediction\cite{katesharbeck,rea,zhu} and in magnetic control\cite{degrave},
and confinement scaling has been revisited with symbolic
regression\cite{murari}, Bayesian neural networks\cite{gao}, tuned tree
ensembles\cite{xiong}, deep ensembles with features aligned across
machines\cite{namseo}, and a Kolmogorov--Arnold network learned as a residual
on a power law\cite{wang}. Measured the usual way, the flexible models win. On
the rows used here, a random forest scores 0.128 in log-RMSE against 0.181 for
a power law refitted in the same folds, 29\% lower, and 0.199 for the
published IPB98(y,2) law, 36\% lower.

That comparison holds out discharges, and every machine in a held-out fold also
appears in the training folds. A held-out row has hundreds of near neighbours
recorded on the same device at nearby settings, so the score measures how much
of JET is predictable from the rest of JET. A scaling law is used on a machine
that is not in the database at all. Fields whose data arrive in clusters match
the validation split to the generalisation being claimed\cite{roberts}:
materials informatics adopted leave-one-cluster-out cross-validation to measure
extrapolation to unseen chemistries\cite{meredig}, and treats distance from the
training data as the applicability domain of a model\cite{sutton}. Inside
fusion, Abbate \emph{et al.}\cite{abbate} make the same argument for transport
models: score them on a large database, against a baseline chosen for the use
they are put to.

This paper applies that principle to confinement scaling on the International
Tokamak Physics Activity (ITPA) database,
and reports four results.
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
\item \emph{The ranking of the models reverses.} Held out one device at a time,
the random forest that wins cross-validation is worse than the refitted power
law on all 13 eligible database labels and all 11 eligible physical devices.
Between a power law and the two tree ensembles, the usual procedure therefore
does more than overstate accuracy: it selects the worst extrapolator.
% ishcdb:begin
The same comparison, run under rules fixed and archived before the data were
downloaded on the stellarator-heliotron confinement database\cite{iss04}, which
shares no device with the tokamak one, reproduces the inversion on every
eligible held-out device.
% ishcdb:end
\item \emph{Discharge-calibrated intervals fail under device shift without
warning.} At a size cut matched to
ITER's step in major radius, split-conformal intervals set to 90\% cover 3\%
and 0.3\% of held-out rows for the two tree ensembles, and their width moves
by under 1.5\%.
\item \emph{Long-range structure improves extrapolation across that cut.}
Connor--Taylor similarity constraints on the exponents and a Gaussian process
with a linear kernel term both reduce the error there, and an ablation that
changes only the long-range mean is consistent with the loss of a continuing
long-range trend as one contributor to the failure, beyond flexibility alone,
in the models tested.
\item \emph{A short reporting convention follows,} and three of its four items
need no refit.
\end{enumerate}

The closest prior work covers parts of this picture. Wang \emph{et al.}\cite{wang},
in a preprint on the same database revision, find that an unanchored network behaves unpredictably
outside the training distribution and that anchoring it to a power law helps.
Their held-out cohorts are defined by thresholds on parameter ranges, inside
the population of machines already seen, and they note that their approach
cannot substitute for device coverage. Butt \emph{et al.}\cite{butt}, also
in a preprint, hold out whole machines on the same standard set and find that adding parameters to a
power law improves the in-sample fit and worsens extrapolation, but they score
no flexible model. Nam and Seo\cite{namseo} build a cross-machine framework,
aligning features across devices and predicting with deep ensembles, and score
it on conditions outside the training set, including JET-ILW and ITER. Gao
\emph{et al.}\cite{gao} fit Bayesian neural networks with uncertainty on the
ITPA database and on single devices, and Xiong \emph{et al.}\cite{xiong}
compare tuned forests and networks with IPB98(y,2) and ITPA20 on the standard
set. Cross-machine prediction, uncertainty-aware models, parameter-defined
shift and extrapolation-oriented power-law selection are therefore not new
here. What this paper adds is narrower: the same model families scored under
discharge-grouped and physical-device-held-out validation on the same
database, the ranking reversal and interval failure that the second split
produces, and a test of which long-range model structures mitigate it. Where
Butt \emph{et al.} find a loss of accuracy inside one family, the finding
here is an inversion between families.

\section{Data and methods}
\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{Data.} The ITPA Global H-mode Confinement Database, revision
DB5.2.3\cite{hdb5}, in the file the Open Science Framework (OSF)
deposit\cite{osfdb} distributes as its standard analysis set STD5. The file
holds 6228 rows from 4471
discharges across 18 device/wall labels, which are 16 physical devices once the
two JET entries and the two ASDEX Upgrade entries are each merged. No row fails
the cleaning rule, which requires every used column to be finite and positive,
and no outlier rule, winsorising or reweighting is applied. The database
description gives STD5 as 7537 points from 18 machines, and the deposit
delivers 6228 under that name. Matching every delivered row to the full
DB5.2.3 file on device, shot and time (Sec.~S18) shows that all 6228 carry
the full file's STD5 flag and all are ELMy H-mode rows. The deposited
revision flags 7568 rows, 31 more than the published count, so exact identity
with the published STD5 membership cannot be established from the deposited
files. The analysed file is the ELMy H-mode rows of the deposited STD5
selection, less 36 rows absent for a reason the files do not record, and it
is analysed as delivered. The target is the
thermal confinement time \texttt{TAUTH}. The file is pinned by SHA-256 and
verified on load, so every number below refers to one set of bytes.

\paragraph{Features and models.} Nine log engineering features: plasma current,
toroidal field, line-averaged density, loss power, major radius, elongation,
inverse aspect ratio, effective mass and minor radius. The candidates are a
ridge regression on the log features with the default unit penalty on
standardised columns, which is a fitted power law (Sec.~\ref{sec:robustness}
repeats the comparison with unpenalised least squares); a random forest of 300
bootstrapped trees\cite{breiman} in which every split considers all nine
features (\texttt{max\_features} of 1.0), so the ensemble bags trees without
feature subsampling; a histogram
gradient booster; log-quadratic and log-cubic ridge
controls; and a mean baseline. The power law and the two tree ensembles are
the primary comparison. Beside the mean baseline, they were the only candidates
in the first committed version of the pipeline, which predates the first
held-out-device analysis in the repository's history. The polynomial and
Gaussian-process models joined with or after that analysis, as controls. None
is tuned for the headline comparison. Every
consequential setting is written out in Sec.~S5 of the supplementary material,
and Sec.~\ref{sec:robustness} reports a nested search. Minor radius is
reconstructed as $a=\epsilon R$ and kept, because a minor radius is what a
modeller would hand a model, so the nine features carry one exact dependency;
the refit of Eq.~\eqref{eq:powerlaw} on the eight independent variables plus
an intercept has condition number 10.7, and Sec.~S12 audits the rank of the
design and the refitted exponents. A tree can split on $a$, $R$ and
$\epsilon$ separately, so the three primary models are also scored on the
eight independent features: the forest still loses on 13 of 13 labels and 11
of 11 devices, at gaps of $+0.240$ and $+0.304$, and scores 0.973 against the
power law's 0.278 across the size cut (Sec.~S9). IPB98(y,2), evaluated analytically, is a historical reference and
not a competitor. It was fitted in 1998 to DB2.8, a predecessor that overlaps
this database heavily in devices and regimes\cite{hdb5}, so it has seen most
of the machines held out below. Its prediction is never used as a feature, and
the like-for-like baseline throughout is the power law refitted inside each
fold.

\paragraph{Splits.} (i) Cross-validation grouped by discharge: five folds of
\texttt{GroupKFold} on the discharge identifier, the device label joined to
the shot number, so the key is unique across machines and every slice of one
discharge falls in the same fold. (ii) Holding out a whole unit, in two
versions. Leave-one-label-out (LOLO) holds out each of the 13 database labels
with at least 30 rows in turn and trains on every other row. Leave-one-device-out
(LODO) holds out each of the 11 physical devices those labels reduce to.
Holding out JET-ILW while JET stays in training does not hold out an unseen
device, so every claim that turns on an unseen device is made on LODO. Merging
the wall eras has a cost of its own, since the ITER-like wall brought a
documented confinement reduction concentrated in the pedestal\cite{leyland}.
The 30-row threshold was fixed before any held-out score was computed and
decides only which labels are scored: the five smaller labels (COMPASS, TCV,
START, TDEV and TFTR, 43 rows between them) stay in every training fold. (iii)
A size-ordered cut, described in Sec.~\ref{sec:size}.

\paragraph{Scores and statistics.} Every model is fitted and scored in
$\log\tau$, and the score is the root-mean-square residual in that variable,
written log-RMSE. Intervals are percentile bootstraps over whole held-out units,
2000 resamples over the 13 labels or the 11 devices; paired intervals resample
the per-unit difference between two models, which removes the difficulty the
units share. The unit behind every claim about an unseen device is the physical
device.
The folds of a leave-one-out arm share almost all of their training rows, so the
units they score are not independent experiments. The bootstrap intervals are
therefore descriptive percentile-bootstrap intervals over held-out units, not
confidence intervals, and the sign-test and permutation $p$-values below are
nominal, descriptive summaries of how lopsided a count is. The claims rest on
the win counts and the size of the gaps.

Table~\ref{tab:reversal} is the central result. Of the three primary models, a
power law, a random forest and a gradient booster, the forest is best under
cross-validation and worst on a label it has not seen. Against the power law
refitted in the same folds its log-RMSE is 29\% lower in the CV column and
117\% higher in the LOLO column. What holds in every partition tested is that the power law
and the two ensembles trade places. The order of the two ensembles between
themselves does not hold: they are 0.002 apart under cross-validation, and over
ten shuffled partitions the forest is ahead of the booster in 2 of 10. The two
polynomial rungs are controls, outside the ranking claim.

The labels differ enormously in difficulty, so the right test is the paired
per-label difference. The random forest is worse than the log-linear power law
on \textbf{13 of 13} database labels, at a mean gap of $+0.251$ with a 95\%
descriptive bootstrap interval of $[+0.157,+0.342]$, and the gradient booster is worse on 12 of 13.

Gaussian process of Sec.~\ref{sec:gp}, and it does not reverse: on a held-out

\subsection{Why the forest fails}
\label{sec:mechanisms}

\paragraph{Distance.} Ranking each model's per-label error against the
Mahalanobis distance of that label's mean log-feature vector from the training
mean gives $\rho=+0.85$ for the random forest (descriptive label-permutation
$p=0.0007$) and
$+0.54$ for the gradient booster, against $\rho=-0.06$ for the log-linear power
law. Over the 11 physical devices the forest's correlation is $+0.77$ and the
power law's is still $-0.06$. The mean baseline, which fits nothing, also
correlates with distance, at $+0.56$. So distance measures how hard a unit is
in general; the difference is that the power law's error does not grow with
it. Mahalanobis distance between mean log-feature vectors is the only
distance used here. It compares first moments through one pooled covariance,
inverted through the pseudo-inverse because the nine features are exactly
collinear (Sec.~S12), so it is blind to differences in the shape of an operating envelope
at a fixed mean. The diagnosis carries over to dimensionless coordinates (Sec.~S10).
Section~S17 gives the permutation tests and the stability of $\rho$ when each
label is dropped, and Sec.~S6 the per-label scores.

\paragraph{A ceiling that binds only once.} A random forest averages leaf
values, and every leaf value is a mean of training targets, so it cannot
predict outside $[\min y_{\text{train}}, \max y_{\text{train}}]$. That bound
binds on one held-out label, JET (circled in the middle panel of
Fig.~\ref{fig:extrap}), where 48\% of the rows exceed the largest confinement
time in the training fold, and across the size cut of
Sec.~\ref{sec:size}, where \textbf{34\%} of held-out rows do. It does not explain
the pattern. The four labels the forest loses by the widest margins are NSTX
($+0.568$), PBX-M ($+0.453$), MAST ($+0.427$) and JFT-2M ($+0.357$), and on each
its ceiling stands about three natural logs above anything the held-out machine
reaches. JET, the one fold where the ceiling is in the way, is seventh of the
thirteen, at $+0.280$. The forest fails hardest where the bound is furthest
from binding, so the failure happens inside the training range: its
predictions stop tracking the features once the input leaves the support it was
fitted on. The gradient booster has no such guarantee, but on every split scored
here its predictions stayed below the training maximum (Sec.~S17).

\paragraph{Flexibility costs the tail.} A polynomial ladder in the log features
(degrees 1 to 3) is flexible and does extrapolate. Degree 2 is not meaningfully
worse than degree 1 on a typical label, at a median of 0.238 against 0.216, but
its worst label scores 1.083 against degree 1's 0.289, and degree 3's worst
scores 4.601 (Fig.~\ref{fig:extrap}, bottom, where the two ensembles are
circled). Every flexible model scored so far is therefore either bounded,
like the two ensembles, or divergent, like the cubic. Nothing yet separates
``flexible models fail out of distribution'' from ``models that stop trending
outside their data fail out of distribution''. Section~\ref{sec:gp} builds the
model that does.

\paragraph{The loss power is the target's denominator.} The thermal
confinement time is the stored energy over the loss power, and the loss power
is one of the nine features. A log-linear model represents that identity
exactly, outside the training range of $P$ as well as inside it, where a tree
cannot. Regressing the stored energy instead keeps $P$ as an ordinary predictor
and removes only the identity. On the 6214 rows that carry a stored energy, the
forest still wins cross-validation, 0.123 against 0.193. Under leave-one-out it
loses on 9 of 13 labels and 9 of 11 devices, at mean gaps of $+0.220$ and
\textbf{$+0.348$}, and across the size cut it scores \textbf{1.081} against the
power law's 0.289. Deleting $P$ outright is the harsher test, because it removes
a real predictor as well, and the forest then loses on 10 of 13 labels and 9 of
11 devices. The identity therefore contributes the unanimity of the 11 of 11
count, and under either control neither nominal sign test reaches $p=0.05$,
but it
does not account for the degradation, which stays at 9 of 11.

\paragraph{Do the features name the machine?} Four of the nine features barely
move once a device is fixed. Major radius carries \textbf{0.1\%} of its log
variance within devices, and a random forest classifier recovers the device
from the nine features at \textbf{99.9\%} under the discharge-grouped split. A
tree can therefore fit a per-device offset under cross-validation that is
worthless on a machine it has not seen. Deleting the near-constant features
removes the reversal, and the forest's interpolation margin grows to 50.3\%,
but that control also deletes the size dependence the power law extrapolates
through, so it cannot tell the two readings apart. Dimensionless coordinates
come closer, and fitting this database in them is established
practice\cite{cordey,hdb5}. In $(\rho^{*},\beta,\nu^{*},q,\epsilon,\kappa,M)$
the size, field and density dependence is kept while no coordinate is a device
label: $\rho^{*}$ moves 26.6\% within a device, against major radius's 0.1\%.
Regressing $B\tau_{E}$ on those seven groups, the forest wins cross-validation
by \textbf{28.9\%} and loses on \textbf{11~of~13} labels and \textbf{9 of 11}
devices. The device gap falls from $+0.315$ to $+0.130$: part of the
engineering-space gap is device identity and part is not. This arm is a
diagnostic rather than a control, because it carries a confound of its own. The temperature the groups need is recovered from the
stored energy as $T\sim W_{\mathrm{th}}/(nV)$ rather than measured, so the
groups are algebraic functions of the target, neither model is blind here in
the way it is in engineering space, and the coordinates cannot place a machine
that does not exist yet. $\epsilon$ and $\kappa$ also still move only 5\% and
6\% within a device. It is a different experiment with a different confound,
not a stricter version of the same one (Sec.~S16). Removing both objections
at once, with stored energy as the target and only the four within-device
features, none of them derived from the target, leaves no reversal: the forest
wins cross-validation, 0.153 against 0.323, and is level with the power law
held out, worse on 5 of 13 labels and 6 of 11 devices at gaps of $+0.002$ and
$+0.050$. That arm deletes the size dependence with the identity, as the
within-device arm does, so it is the harshest control here, and what it shows
is that the reversal lives in the features that carry size, which on this
database also carry identity. This database does not cleanly separate device
identity from size physics.

\paragraph{Other choices.} None of the remaining choices a reader could suspect
carries the reversal (Sec.~S9). With unpenalised least squares as the
comparator instead of ridge, the two power laws differ by 0.0001 in mean
held-out score, and the forest still loses on 13 of 13 labels and 11 of 11
devices, the booster on 12 of 13 and 10 of 11. The power law
wins every scored label at
eligibility thresholds of 20, 30 and 50 rows, and 14 of 15 at 10. Over ten
shuffled fold assignments the forest's cross-validation margin stays between
27.0\% and 28.5\%. A nested hyperparameter search improves the forest's
held-out label score from 0.465 to 0.403, or 0.376 with the inner folds grouped
by machine, against the power law's 0.214. Dropping the spherical tokamaks
leaves the forest worse on all 11 remaining labels. A mixed model with a
random intercept per device, the estimator the clustered-validation literature
pairs with this split, scores \textbf{0.829} per device against the power law's
0.212 (Sec.~S8). On the full DB5.2.3 revision, on 5358 H-mode rows STD5 does
not contain and 3860 ohmic and L-mode rows, a tree ensemble loses to the
refitted power law on an unseen label in \textbf{16 of 24} label-model pairs
and \textbf{9 of 10} (Sec.~S2).

\section{A size cut matched to ITER}
\label{sec:size}

Holding out JET still leaves the rest of the database spanning much of its
size range, so leave-one-out extrapolates in identity while interpolating in
size. ITER's major radius is \SI{6.2}{\meter} against \SI{3.40}{\meter} for the
largest row here, a factor of 1.82, and that factor is available inside the
database. Training on the 14 smallest database labels (up to DIII-D,
\SI{1.865}{\meter}, 3498 rows) and predicting the 4 largest (up to JT-60U,
\SI{3.40}{\meter}, 2730 rows) asks for a ratio of 1.823 against ITER's 1.824.
The cut is chosen by that ratio, not by eye. The name ITER-size-matched, used
in the tables and figures, means matched in that ratio and in nothing else;
the end of this section and Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations} say what else the cut
moves.

Table~\ref{tab:escalation} sets the cut beside the two easier splits. The power

The random forest scores 0.938 and the gradient booster 1.072, so the two models

is weaker than IPB98(y,2)'s is an active question\cite{hall,hall26}, and across
this cut $\log\rho^{*}$ and $\log\nu^{*}$ separate as well as size does
(Sec.~S10). The cut is therefore a joint step in size, normalised gyroradius and
collisionality, and no claim rests on it alone. The claim that rests on many
machines is the leave-one-device-out result of Sec.~\ref{sec:reversal}.

\section{Discharge-calibrated intervals that miss under device shift without widening}
\label{sec:conformal}

For a next-step device the point error is not the deliverable; the interval is.
Split-conformal intervals\cite{conformal} are calibrated here on absolute log
residuals, holding back 25\% of the \emph{discharges} in each training fold,
and the half-width is the finite-sample conformal rank of those scores at
$\alpha=0.10$ (Sec.~S5 gives the construction). Kardaun\cite{kardaun} built
confidence and prediction intervals for ITER's confinement time from the
predecessor database by a parametric route. The question here is narrower:
whether an interval calibrated the usual way still covers once the held-out
unit becomes a database label, and then a physical device.

multiplicative half-width under leave-one-out.}

Model & CV & LOLO & ITER cut & width \\

\end{table}

Under grouped cross-validation every model covers within a point of 90\%
(Table~\ref{tab:coverage}). On an unseen label the random forest's 90\% interval
covers 35\% of rows, and across the ITER-size-matched cut 3\%. The gradient
booster covers \textbf{8} of the 2730 rows there. A calibration bug would give
the same number, so it is worth checking by hand. The booster's half-width
across the cut is 0.221 in logs, and its per-label errors above the cut run from
0.66 to 1.13, three to five times the half-width, so its intervals have no
chance of containing the held-out values. Every coverage in
Table~\ref{tab:coverage} pools rows. With each held-out label weighted equally
instead, the forest's 35\% becomes 26\% and the booster's 45\% becomes 41\%.
Above the cut, neither tree ensemble's interval covers more than 6\% of any
label. Cross-validation over the 13 scored labels stays within two points of
nominal for every model, so the collapse does not depend on the aggregation.

The widths barely move. No model's median half-width changes by more than
1.5\% between the two arms. The construction has no mechanism that depends on
the test covariates, so it cannot widen for a more distant prediction; the
near-constancy observed here is empirical, since the calibration scores are
drawn afresh in each arm, rather than guaranteed by the conformal rank
formula. What is measured is the coverage.
The fixed width is the practical problem, because the interval carries no sign
that it has stopped covering. The assumption that fails is exchangeability,
which the weighted-conformal literature can restore only by knowing the
shift\cite{shiftcp}, and calibrated uncertainty degrading under dataset shift is
a general finding rather than a fusion one\cite{ovadia}. Coverage falls with
distance for the trees, at $\rho=-0.77$ for the forest and $-0.54$ for the
booster, and not for the power law, at $+0.27$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:conformal}). Rows
arrive in correlated clusters of slices from one discharge, so every coverage
here is an empirical rate rather than a guaranteed one.

\centering

If the collapse is about exchangeability, calibrating on held-out labels
instead of held-out discharges should restore coverage, and only as far as
that unit reaches. Both halves hold empirically; no finite-sample guarantee is
claimed for either. On a held-out label, coverage returns to within two
points of nominal for every model, the random forest going from 35\% to 88\%.
Held out by physical device, with the wall variants collapsed onto the 11
devices, plain split-conformal coverage is 31\% for the forest and 38\% for
the booster (Sec.~S1), below the 35\% and 45\% on a held-out label.
Across the ITER-size-matched cut none of the schemes tested restores it,
because every calibration unit is smaller than the test unit, and scaling the
interval by distance lifts the forest only from 3\% to 40\%. Calibrated by
device, pooled over rows, the restoration is less complete still: the booster
reaches 77\%, or 93\% with each device weighted equally, and the trees buy
their coverage with multiplicative half-widths of 2.3 to 2.8, where the
linear models stay near 1.4.
The constrained power law of Sec.~\ref{sec:dimensional} is the only refitted
model whose plain split-conformal coverage stays near nominal at the size cut,
at 91\% (Sec.~S1).

\section{What extrapolates: long-range structure}
\label{sec:structure}

\subsection{Connor--Taylor constraints on the exponents}
\label{sec:dimensional}

Every model to this point learns its functional form from the data. Connor and
Taylor\cite{connor} showed that requiring a confinement scaling law to be
expressible in the dimensionless plasma variables imposes \emph{linear equality
constraints on its exponents}, and dimensionless-parameter scaling experiments
work in the same transformation family\cite{petty,luce}. A physics assumption
is then a matrix $C$, and the fit becomes
\begin{equation}
\min_{b}\ \lVert Xb - y \rVert^{2} \quad \text{subject to} \quad Cb = d ,
\end{equation}
which the Karush--Kuhn--Tucker system solves in closed form. The constraints are
derived in code from the definitions of the groups, written as exponents on
(length, field, temperature, density),
\begin{equation}
\rho^{*} \sim T^{1/2} B^{-1} L^{-1}, \qquad
\beta \sim n\,T\,B^{-2}, \qquad
\nu^{*} \sim n\,L\,T^{-2}.
\label{eq:groups}
\end{equation}
A law expressible in whichever groups a model holds fixed must be invariant
under every scale transformation that leaves them unchanged, and each such
transformation is one row of $C$. Holding $\rho^{*}$, $\beta$ and $\nu^{*}$
fixed (Kadomtsev\cite{kadomtsev}) gives one row, holding $\rho^{*}$ and $\beta$
(collisionless) two, and holding $\rho^{*}$ alone (electrostatic) three
(Sec.~S13).

IPB98(y,2) is itself an instance: the high-$\beta$ Kadomtsev constraint was
enforced when it was fitted, with the field exponent held at
0.15\cite{ipb98,hdb5}. Its exponents lie within 0.00096 of the Kadomtsev surface
derived here, which checks the derivation. It may also be part of why a law
fitted in 1998 to a predecessor database transfers as well as it does. On a held-out
label IPB98(y,2) scores 0.188 against 0.214 for the unconstrained refit on the
same rows and features, and at the size cut 0.194 against 0.278. The newer
ITPA20 laws sit further from the Kadomtsev and collisionless surfaces and
transfer better still, so closeness to a surface does not order laws fitted
to different populations (Sec.~S13). That distance is computed on the
published engineering-form exponents; the dimensionless forms the ITPA fitted
directly obey the high-$\beta$ Kadomtsev relation well\cite{yoshida25}, and
transforming an engineering law algebraically accumulates the uncertainty of
every exponent, so it is not a claim that those laws violate the similarity.
What the hierarchy tests is narrower: a refit on these rows in which the
constraint is the only difference.

The Kadomtsev rung is \emph{literature-fixed}. Kadomtsev specified it in 1975,
and it was enforced when IPB98(y,2) was fitted, so nothing about it was
selected here; it was not, however, registered before the size-cut scores
existed in the way the external replication of Sec.~S15 was, and all three
rungs were scored in one run.
It is free: at 0.1808 in sample it matches the unconstrained fit to four
decimals, because the data already satisfy it. It still beats the unconstrained
fit at every one of the 15 cuts in the size sweep, including all 8 well-powered
ones, those that train on at least 1000 rows. At six training machines the
unconstrained fit scores 0.971 and the Kadomtsev fit 0.274. A constraint stops a
fit wandering along the weak direction of the design when the training half is
small or shifted. Where it is neither, on the leave-one-out arms, the rung gains
0.004 by label and 0.001 by device, inside the resolution of

The collisionless rung is the stronger assumption and the better score. It costs

\subsection{Flexibility or long-range saturation?}
\label{sec:gp}

Flexibility is how much structure a model can learn beyond a power law.
\emph{Saturation} is whether its predictions stop tracking the features once the
input leaves the training support. The forest is provably confined to its
training range, the booster never left it, and a squared-exponential Gaussian
process reverts to its prior mean. The ensembles are flexible and saturating,
ridge is neither, and the cubic is flexible and non-saturating but divergent. A
Gaussian process\cite{gpml} with a linear-plus-RBF kernel, a linear term added
to a squared-exponential (radial-basis-function, RBF) term, is flexible,
non-saturating and well behaved at long range. The tool is used in this field
to fit plasma profiles\cite{chilenski}; the kernel is chosen here for its
behaviour far outside the data. Three kernels fitted over the same features with
the same optimiser (Sec.~S4) order as saturation predicts. The
squared-exponential kernel alone scores 1.948 at the ITER-size-matched cut, worse
than a constant, with an error tracking distance at $\rho=+0.65$. Adding a
linear term gives the lowest cross-validated score in this study, 0.112 against
the forest's 0.128, with 0.191 at the cut and an error flat against distance, at
$\rho=-0.01$.

A linear term enlarges the function class as well as changing the asymptote, so
that ladder suggests the mechanism without isolating it. The ablation of
Table~\ref{tab:meanfunction} tests it more directly. Write the model as a mean
function
plus a residual process,
\begin{equation}
\log\tau = m(x) + g_{\text{RBF}}(x),
\label{eq:meanfunction}
\end{equation}
and hold the covariance family, tuning subsample, initialisation, seed and
optimiser fixed across the arms, re-optimising the hyperparameters within each;
only $m$ is changed by hand. With a constant mean the model
reproduces the squared-exponential rung to every digit computed, which is the
control. A fitted power-law mean moves the cut from 1.948 to 0.278, a factor of
7.0, and 0.278 is plain ridge's score there to six decimals: far from the data
the residual process has decayed and the model returns its mean. A third arm
keeps that from being a tautology. Its mean also trends, with IPB98(y,2)'s
published exponents, fitted to nothing here. Swapping it in moves the cut to
\textbf{0.186} and the held-out label score from 0.212 to 0.189. Within this
kernel family, keeping a continuing long-range trend improves extrapolation
substantially, and the form of the trend changes the answer materially.

\begin{ruledtabular}

Across the models examined here, then, the failures are more consistent with
the loss of a continuing long-range trend than with flexibility alone. The
experiment does not establish saturation as a unique or general cause of
extrapolation failure: Sec.~\ref{sec:robustness} shows that device identity in
the features and the loss power in the target each carry part of the
engineering-space margin. The practical rule concerns saturation: avoid models
whose predictions stop tracking the features once the input leaves the
training data, whether or not they are flexible. The
process does not displace the constrained power law: across the cut it scores
0.191 against 0.183, a tie at the resolution of Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations}, and
held out one physical device at a time it scores 0.219 against the power law's
0.211, worse on 5 of the 11. What it adds is a native posterior interval that
was not conformally recalibrated. At the ITER-size-matched cut its nominal
90\% interval covers 92.5\% of rows, where split-conformal intervals give the
random forest 3\% and the gradient booster 0.3\%. That is one split's empirical
rate, not a coverage guarantee for a new device, and it carries no allowance
for the choice of kernel or the fitted hyperparameters. The
squared-exponential rung is the
instructive failure. It is the one model that does widen out of distribution,
with a half-width four times any other, and it still covers only 16.7\%,
because it has reverted to a prior mean nowhere near the answer. A wider
interval does not help when it is centred in the wrong place.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Conventional within-database evaluation does not measure what a scaling law is
used for. Cross-validation grouped by discharge, shot or time slice holds out
rows from machines that stay in the training set, so it measures interpolation
within known devices. On this database the two comparisons do not merely differ
in size; they order the candidate models in opposite directions, and a gain
reported under the first does not establish a gain under the second. Among the
three primary models the convention picks the worst extrapolator. The failure
depends on the candidate set. When the set includes a model whose predictions
keep trending, such as the linear-plus-RBF process, cross-validation selects
it, and on a held-out device it stays level with the power law. Adding that
model removes the failure for this candidate set. It does not show that
discharge-grouped cross-validation selects reliably for unseen devices in
general. Two of the controls bound the headline. The loss power appears in
both the target, $\tau_E=W/P$, and the predictors, and that identity
contributes to the unanimity of the 11 of 11 count: with stored energy as the
target the deterioration remains but is 9 of 11. And the engineering features
nearly name the machine; deleting the near-constant ones removes the reversal
along with the size dependence, and the dimensionless diagnostic that restores
it is not blind to the target. The reversal is robust as a warning about this
database and this way of evaluating models; the mechanism behind it is less
settled than the count. Tree ensembles are fitted
to this database\cite{xiong}; whether Bayesian\cite{gao} or
feature-aligned\cite{namseo} networks saturate is a question about those
architectures that only this split would settle, and none of them is scored
here.

\subsection{What this changes in practice}
\label{sec:checklist}

The consequence is mainly a reporting convention. For any confinement model
offered as an alternative to a power law:
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{3pt}
\item Report leave-one-device-out beside cross-validation. It costs one refit per
device, and where the two disagree, as here by a factor of 3.6, the
disagreement is the result.
\item Report how far the target operating point sits from the training
distribution, the one quantity known in advance for a machine that does not
exist. What separates the models is the contrast, $\rho=+0.85$ for the random
forest against $\rho=-0.06$ for the power law, since even the mean baseline
correlates with distance.
\item Say whether the model's predictions keep tracking the features outside
the training support, and whether they can leave the range of the training
targets at all; a random forest provably cannot. Both are known before
anything is fitted.
\item Do not carry a calibrated interval across a device boundary without saying
so, and do not read its width as evidence. Here 90\% nominal delivered 35\% on
an unseen label and 3\% across the size cut, at a width that moved by under
1.5\%.
\end{enumerate}
\noindent Only the first needs a refit.

The positive results point the same way. A Connor--Taylor constraint and a
linear kernel term are unrelated operations, and both supply long-range
structure. For practice, the cheaper model was also the stronger one: a power
law refitted on the same rows costs one least-squares solve and is a stronger
baseline out of distribution than any tree ensemble scored here. Where a flexible model is wanted, the
evidence favours one that keeps trending. On this database the
literature-fixed Kadomtsev constraint is free in sample and the collisionless rung costs 0.001,
inside the resolution of Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations}; only the electrostatic
rung costs more than that.

What the models predict for SPARC, JT-60SA and ITER is locked in Sec.~S3 of
the supplementary material, with a lock date of 31 August 2026 and a content
digest over the input rows; it is a record of predictions, not a validation.
On JT-60SA, inside the database's size range, all five models agree to within
15\%. On ITER, $1.82\times$ beyond it, they disagree by a factor of 8.3, and
the random forest cannot close its gap, because its prediction cannot exceed
the largest training target, \SI{1.321}{\second}. The constrained power law
sits \textbf{21\%} below IPB98(y,2) at the historical $Q=10$ reference point of
the 1999 basis\cite{ipb98}, which ITER has since rebaselined\cite{loarte};
that estimate comes from a rung selected on the size-cut scores. SPARC sits
further from the training data than ITER, at a Mahalanobis distance of 6.9
against 4.7, because its field is 2.1 times the largest in the database.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\emph{Resolution.} Two models whose per-label scores differ by less than about
0.01 in log-RMSE are tied here; that is roughly the bootstrap spread of one
label's score and what a fold assignment moves the cross-validated margin by.
The orderings this paper claims, such as the forest's 0.465 against 0.214 and
the size cut's 0.938 against 0.278, are an order of magnitude clear of it.

\emph{One machine above the cut.} The ITER-size-matched cut reproduces ITER's
size ratio and nothing else: ITER is a burning plasma dominated by alpha
heating at a gain no row here approaches, so clearing this bar is necessary and
not sufficient. Of the 2730 held-out rows, 2628 are one physical device. The
interval collapse, the bounded correction, the constrained fit's 0.183, the
Gaussian process's 0.191 and the mean-function ablation are all scored across
it, so all five are one-machine evidence about a large jump. A second large
device at comparable weight is what the comparison needs, and the database does
not contain one.

\emph{Population and weighting.} JET and ASDEX Upgrade with their variants
supply 77\% of rows, and every fit weights rows equally. Weighting each label
equally costs the power law more than the forest and takes the win count to 12
of 13 (Sec.~S9). Only 13 of the 18 labels are scored, and the five left out are
the smallest.

\emph{Selection.} Across the splits, published laws, fitted models, constraint
rungs, kernels, mean functions, size cuts, thresholds, partitions, feature sets
and targets, this paper reports of order forty out-of-distribution comparisons,
with no correction for multiplicity; every $p$-value among them is a nominal
sign or permutation test over at most thirteen dependent units. The descriptive gaps that carry the paper are
far clear of its resolution, but its constructive results come from a search.
The Kadomtsev rung was specified from prior physics rather than selected by
searching the hierarchy, though it was scored alongside the other rungs; the
collisionless rung, the damping of the bounded correction and the interval
scheme of the locked forecast were each chosen after a held-out score had been
seen, and all of them should be read as exploratory.

\emph{Errors in variables.} Every power-law fit treats the engineering
parameters as exact, which the confinement-scaling literature has long objected
to\cite{kardaun,cordey,gls}. Orthogonal distance regression on this design runs
away along its weak direction, so no errors-in-variables fit is offered
(Sec.~S14).

\emph{Model classes.} The mixed model has a single random intercept and no
random slopes (Sec.~S8). The Gaussian-process intervals are equal-tailed
posterior intervals on $\log\tau$ that include the fitted noise term, the
closest analogue of a conformal interval but not the same construction, and the
kernels are tuned on a seeded subsample of each training fold (Sec.~S4). The
Gaussian-process result is for one kernel family,
and a process with a linear part constrained as in Sec.~\ref{sec:dimensional},
which would combine the two things that work here, is untested. Of the three locked forecasts, only JT-60SA's can be checked in the near term,
and only once it runs an H-mode campaign.

\section{Conclusion}

On real confinement data, the model with 29\% lower cross-validated log-RMSE
than a log-linear power law refitted in the same folds loses to that power law
on all
13 eligible held-out database labels and on all 11 eligible physical devices.
A control for the loss power in the target and a diagnostic in dimensionless
coordinates remove the unanimity of that count but not the effect, although
the diagnostic is not blind to the target; deleting the features that carry
size, and with them device identity, removes the reversal.
% ishcdb:begin
The inversion reproduces on a stellarator-heliotron database under rules fixed
before its data were seen.
% ishcdb:end
At an internal cut of the database chosen to match ITER's 1.82-fold step in
major radius, the two tree ensembles land closer to a constant predictor than
to the power law, and their nominal 90\% intervals cover 3\% and 0.3\% of
held-out rows at unchanged width.

Two interventions that supplied long-range structure improved extrapolation
across that internal stress test: a Connor--Taylor constraint on the
exponents, whose Kadomtsev rung costs nothing in sample, and a Gaussian
process whose mean keeps trending. Across the models tested, poor
extrapolation goes with predictions that stop tracking the features once the
input leaves the training data, not with flexibility as such; device identity
in the features and the loss power in the target each carry part of the
margin, so the count is firmer than the mechanism. A scaling law is asked for
a number at a point no row occupies, so model selection for a next device
should include whole-device holdout beside within-device validation, since a
device is what the deployment will not have seen.
